Will we see our loved ones again in heaven?

If you've ever stood at a graveside and watched them lower the casket of someone you loved, you've asked that question. Maybe not out loud. Maybe only in the car on the way home, or late at night when the house is too quiet. Will I see her again? Will I hear his voice again? Will my mother know me? Will I get to hold that baby I lost?

Last time we were together, we looked at what the Bible says happens to a believer's soul the moment they die. We saw that, for the child of God, to be absent from the body is to be present with the Lord. And at the end, I promised we would come back to this question, because so many of you wrote to ask it.

So today, let's open the Word of God together and look, Scripture by Scripture, at what the Bible tells us about seeing our loved ones in heaven. Will we know them? Will they know us? Will we remember our life together? What about husbands and wives? What about children? And what about the people we love who may not have known the Lord?

I'm going to be honest with you. There are some things the Bible tells us plainly, and some things it doesn't tell us in detail. I won't make up anything to make you feel better. I don't need to. Because what the Bible does tell us is more than enough to dry a lot of tears.

So get your Bible, your reading glasses, and something warm to drink. Let's sit together for a while.

I'm Pastor Samuel, and I'm so glad you're here.

Let's begin with one of the saddest moments in the life of King David.

Turn to Second Samuel, chapter twelve. David had sinned greatly, and the prophet Nathan had come to confront him. And the child born to David and Bathsheba became very sick.

For seven days, David fasted and prayed. He lay on the ground all night long. His servants tried to lift him up and get him to eat, and he refused. He was pleading with God for the life of his little boy.

And on the seventh day, the child died.

The servants were afraid to tell him. They said, if he was like this while the child was alive, what will he do now? But David saw them whispering, and he knew. And then he did something that shocked them all. He got up off the ground. He washed. He changed his clothes. He went into the house of the Lord and worshipped. And then he sat down and ate.

His servants couldn't understand it. And David explained, in verse twenty-three:

"But now he is dead, wherefore should I fast? can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me."

I shall go to him.

David couldn't bring his son back to this world. But he knew, with a quiet certainty, that one day he would go to where his son was. And that hope was strong enough to lift a grieving father off the floor.

If you've lost a child, a grandchild, or a baby you never got to hold, I want you to hear those words. I shall go to him. David didn't say, I shall go to a place where there are souls I don't know. He said, I shall go to him. To my boy.

Now let's go back even further, to the very first book of the Bible.

When the Bible describes the death of the great men of faith in Genesis, it uses a beautiful phrase. Listen to how it describes the death of Abraham, in Genesis twenty-five, verse eight:

"Then Abraham gave up the ghost, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people."

Gathered to his people.

Now, Abraham wasn't buried with his people. His family was buried far away, in Ur and in Haran. Abraham was buried in a cave in Canaan. So "gathered to his people" isn't talking about a family cemetery. It's talking about something more. The same words are used for Isaac, for Jacob, for Aaron, and for Moses. Even in those early days, God's people understood that death was not just being put in the ground. It was being gathered. Brought home. Reunited with those who had gone before.

I love that word, gathered. It's what a mother does with her children when a storm is coming. It's what a farmer does with his harvest. Nothing lost. Everything brought in.

Now let's come to the New Testament, and to one of the most amazing scenes in all the Gospels.

Turn to Matthew, chapter seventeen. Jesus took Peter, James, and John up into a high mountain. And there, the Bible says, He was transfigured before them. His face shone like the sun, and His clothes were white as the light.

And then, verse three:

"And, behold, there appeared unto them Moses and Elias talking with him."

Elias is Elijah. Now think about this. Moses had died about fifteen hundred years earlier. Elijah had been taken up to heaven about nine hundred years earlier. And yet here they are, on that mountain, talking with Jesus.

They were still Moses and Elijah. They hadn't become somebody else. They hadn't lost who they were. They were aware, they were talking, and they knew what was going on. Luke's Gospel tells us they were speaking with Jesus about His coming death in Jerusalem.

But here's the part I want you to notice. Look at verse four:

"Then answered Peter, and said unto Jesus, Lord, it is good for us to be here: if thou wilt, let us make here three tabernacles; one for thee, and one for Moses, and one for Elias."

How did Peter know who they were?

There were no photographs in those days. Peter had never seen a painting of Moses or Elijah. Nobody had introduced them. And yet Peter knew them right away. Moses. Elijah.

Many Bible teachers believe this shows us something about heaven. That in the presence of the Lord, we won't know less than we know now. We'll know more. If Peter, still in his earthly body, could recognize men he had never met, how much more will we recognize the people we've loved and lived with all our lives?

Now turn with me to First Corinthians, chapter thirteen. This is the love chapter, the one so many of us had read at our weddings. And near the end, in verse twelve, Paul says this:

"For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known."

Now I know in part. But then I shall know.

Paul is saying that our knowledge in heaven won't shrink. It will grow. Right now, we see things dimly, like looking at our reflection in an old, cloudy mirror. But then, face to face. We will know fully, the way God knows us fully.

Some of you are worried that heaven will be a place where you forget everything. Where you float around like a cloud, with no memory of who you were or who you loved. That is not the picture the Bible gives us. The Bible says we'll know more, not less.

Let's look at another passage that tells us something about recognition after death. Turn to Luke, chapter sixteen. We looked at this last time. Jesus told about a rich man and a poor beggar named Lazarus who both died.

Listen to verse twenty-three:

"And in hell he lift up his eyes, being in torments, and seeth Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom."

Now, I told you last time that we have to be careful with this story. Jesus told it to teach about how we use our riches and about the urgency of repentance. It's not a detailed map of the afterlife. But in this story, Jesus tells us that after death, the rich man recognized Lazarus. The beggar who used to lie at his gate. And he recognized Abraham, too. And he remembered his life on earth. He remembered his five brothers, and he was worried about them.

Jesus told this story. And in His story, people after death still know who they are, still recognize others, and still remember their families. I don't believe the Lord Jesus would put something in His teaching that would mislead us about this.

Now let me show you something tender.

Turn to John, chapter twenty. It's the first Easter morning. Mary Magdalene has come to the tomb while it's still dark, and she finds the stone rolled away and the body of Jesus gone. She thinks someone has taken Him. And she's standing outside the tomb, weeping.

Then she turns around and sees a man standing there. She thinks He's the gardener. And she says, "Sir, if thou have borne him hence, tell me where thou hast laid him, and I will take him away."

And then, verse sixteen:

"Jesus saith unto her, Mary. She turned herself, and saith unto him, Rabboni; which is to say, Master."

He said her name.

Just one word. Mary. And she knew Him instantly. She knew His voice.

I believe that's how it's going to be for so many of us. Not a long introduction. Not a name tag. Just the sound of a voice we know, saying our name. And we'll turn around, and we'll know.

And after His resurrection, Jesus was still recognizable as Himself. When He appeared to His disciples, He said, in Luke twenty-four, verse thirty-nine:

"Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have."

It is I myself. The same Jesus. Glorified, yes, changed, yes, but still Himself. And the Bible tells us that our resurrection bodies will be like His. In Philippians three, verse twenty-one, Paul says the Lord Jesus "shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body."

So if Jesus was still recognizably Himself after the resurrection, we can trust that we will be too. Your mother will still be your mother. Your husband will still be your husband. Your friend will still be your friend. Only better. Healed. Whole. Without sin or sickness or sorrow.

Some of you have asked me another question about this. "Pastor, what will they look like? Will my husband look the way he did at the end, when he was so sick and thin? Will my mother be old, or young?"

The Bible doesn't tell us how old anyone will look in heaven, and I won't pretend it does. But it does tell us what kind of bodies we'll have at the resurrection. Turn to First Corinthians fifteen, verses forty-two through forty-four:

"So also is the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body."

Sown in weakness, raised in power.

If the last memory you have of your loved one is a hospital bed, a wheelchair, an oxygen tube, or a face that didn't recognize you anymore because of dementia, I want you to hold onto that verse. That is not how you will see them again. What was sown in weakness will be raised in power. What was sown in dishonor will be raised in glory.

The one who couldn't remember your name will know you fully. The one who couldn't walk will run. The one whose voice grew weak will sing.

Now let me show you the verse I believe settles it more than any other.

Turn to First Thessalonians, chapter four. We read this last time too. The believers in Thessalonica were grieving their loved ones who had died, and Paul wrote to comfort them. Listen again to verse seventeen:

"Then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air: and so shall we ever be with the Lord."

Together with them.

Paul was writing to grieving people about their own loved ones who had died. And his comfort was this: you're going to be together with them. Not near them. Not in the same general area. Together with them.

If we didn't recognize each other, if we were all strangers in heaven, then that promise would be no comfort at all. Paul could have just said, you'll be with the Lord. And that would be wonderful. But he specifically said, together with them. Because he knew that's what a grieving heart needs to hear.

And then he says, in verse eighteen, "Wherefore comfort one another with these words."

Paul was looking forward to his own reunions too. Turn back a couple of chapters, to First Thessalonians, chapter two, verses nineteen and twenty. Paul is talking about the believers he led to the Lord in that city:

"For what is our hope, or joy, or crown of rejoicing? Are not even ye in the presence of our Lord Jesus Christ at his coming? For ye are our glory and joy."

Paul expected to see those people again. He expected to know them. And he said they would be his joy.

Can you imagine it? The Sunday school teacher who taught you about Jesus when you were seven years old. The grandmother who prayed for you every night. The friend who invited you to church. The pastor who baptized you. One day, you'll see them again, in the presence of the Lord, and you'll be able to thank them.

And Jesus Himself told us about a great gathering in His kingdom. In Matthew eight, verse eleven, He said:

"Many shall come from the east and west, and shall sit down with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, in the kingdom of heaven."

Sit down with them. That's a family table, friend. That's a homecoming dinner. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, still known by their names, sitting down with people from every corner of the earth.

Jesus also told us that He Himself is preparing the place where we'll be gathered. In John fourteen, verse two, He said, "In my Father's house are many mansions: if it were not so, I would have told you. I go to prepare a place for you."

My Father's house. Not a hotel full of strangers. A family home. Jesus used the most familiar picture He could find: a father's house, with room for all the children.

And the writer of Hebrews tells us that the faithful men and women of old were looking forward to that very place. Hebrews eleven, verse sixteen:

"But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he hath prepared for them a city."

He hath prepared for them a city. A real place, prepared by God, for a real people who know Him and know each other.

Now, I know there are some harder questions in your heart. Let me take them one at a time, as honestly as I can.

The first one is about marriage. Many of you have been married forty, fifty, sixty years. And some of you are now widows and widowers, and you ask me, "Pastor, will I still be married to my husband in heaven? Will my wife still be my wife?"

Jesus was asked a question about this. Some men came to Him with a trick question about a woman who had been married seven times. Whose wife would she be in the resurrection? And Jesus answered, in Matthew twenty-two, verse thirty:

"For in the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God in heaven."

Now, when some people hear that, their hearts sink. They think it means they'll lose the person they loved most. But I don't believe that's what Jesus meant.

Marriage on this earth is a picture. The Bible tells us that it's a picture of Christ and His church. And in heaven, we'll have the real thing that the picture was always pointing to. There will be no need for new marriages, no need for weddings, no need to have children or to build a household. But nothing in that verse says you will forget the one you loved, or that your love will disappear.

Think about it this way. Everything good about your marriage, the faithfulness, the kindness, the laughter, the way you took care of each other through sickness and hard times, all of that came from God in the first place. God doesn't throw away good things. He perfects them.

I believe that in heaven, you'll love your husband or your wife more than you ever did on earth, not less. Without the selfishness. Without the arguments. Without the misunderstandings. You'll love each other with a pure love, and you'll both love the Lord more than anything. And you'll be together with Him forever.

The second question is about children. Many of you have lost a baby, or a child, or a grandchild, before they were old enough to understand the gospel. And you wonder, where are they?

We already read David's words about his little son. "I shall go to him." David, a man after God's own heart, was confident that he would see that child again.

The Bible doesn't give us every detail about little ones who die. But it does show us the heart of Jesus toward children. In Mark chapter ten, when the disciples tried to keep the children away, Jesus was much displeased, and He said, "Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God." And He took them up in His arms, put His hands on them, and blessed them.

Friend, that's the same Jesus who holds your little one now. You can trust Him with that child.

The third question is the hardest one. What about the people I love who didn't seem to know the Lord?

I won't pretend this is easy. The Bible is clear that salvation comes only through Jesus Christ. And it would not be loving of me to tell you something different just to make you feel better.

But I want to tell you two things.

First, you don't know everything that happened in their heart. You weren't there for every moment. You weren't there in the hospital at three o'clock in the morning when they were alone with God. Remember the thief on the cross. He turned to Jesus in the very last hours of his life, and Jesus said, "To day shalt thou be with me in paradise." Nobody watching from the crowd that day would have thought that man was going to heaven. Only God sees the heart.

Second, you can trust the character of God. Abraham asked, in Genesis eighteen, verse twenty-five, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" And the answer is yes. God is perfectly just, and He is perfectly merciful. He loves your loved one far more than you ever could. You can leave them in His hands.

And let me add this, very gently. If there are people in your life right now who don't know the Lord, while they're still here, while there's still time, tell them. Not with pressure. Not with fear. With love. Tell them what Jesus means to you. Pray for them by name. Some of the greatest reunions in heaven will be with people who came to faith because someone who loved them never stopped praying.

The fourth question people ask is this: if I remember my loved ones, will I remember the sad things too? Won't I be sad in heaven about the people who aren't there, or about the painful things in my life?

Here's what the Bible promises. Revelation twenty-one, verse four:

"And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away."

I don't know exactly how God will do it. But I know He promises that there will be no more sorrow. Whatever we remember, it will not hurt us anymore. Whatever we know, it will not break our hearts. God Himself will wipe away every tear.

And the psalmist tells us what fills the place where sorrow used to be. Psalm sixteen, verse eleven:

"Thou wilt shew me the path of life: in thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore."

Fulness of joy. Not partial joy. Not joy mixed with regret. Fulness of joy.

Now, there's one more thing I need to say, and it's important.

As wonderful as it will be to see our loved ones again, they are not the center of heaven. Jesus is.

The best part of heaven is not the reunion with your mother, as precious as that will be. The best part of heaven is seeing the face of the One who loved you and gave Himself for you. And here's the beautiful thing. Your loved ones who died in the Lord already know that. They're not sitting around missing you the way you miss them. They are worshipping the Lord. And when you get there, you'll worship Him together.

Revelation seven, verses nine and ten, gives us a glimpse of it:

"After this I beheld, and, lo, a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands; And cried with a loud voice, saying, Salvation to our God which sitteth upon the throne, and unto the Lamb."

I believe your loved ones are in that multitude. And one day, you'll be standing right beside them, singing that same song.

Let me say a word to those of you who feel guilty for still being here. Maybe your husband or your wife went first, and some days you feel like you should have gone with them. Maybe you wonder why God left you behind.

Friend, God has not left you behind. As long as you are here, He has a purpose for you here. Your loved one finished their race. You are still running yours. And one of the best ways to honor the people you've lost is to keep running well.

Tell your grandchildren the stories. Show them the old pictures. Tell them how their great-grandmother prayed, how their grandfather read his Bible every morning at the kitchen table. Write some of it down while you still remember it. Keep the faith alive in the next generation, the way someone kept it alive for you.

So what does all this mean for us, today, while we're still here?

First, it means you can grieve with hope. You can cry. You should cry. Jesus wept at the tomb of His friend. But you don't have to grieve like someone who has no hope. Your goodbye is not forever. For the believer, it's "see you later."

Second, it means you should make sure of your own salvation. All of these promises belong to those who are in Christ. If you want to see your believing loved ones again, the most important thing you can do is put your own trust in Jesus. Your mother didn't pray for you all those years for nothing. She wanted to see you there.

Third, it means you should love people well while you have them. Call your sister. Forgive your brother. Tell your children and grandchildren that you love them, and tell them about your faith. Don't leave anything unsaid.

And fourth, it means you can look forward. Some of you are tired. Your body hurts. You've buried more people than you can count, and some mornings it's hard to get out of bed. But friend, the best is not behind you. The best is in front of you.

Paul knew what it was to grow weak in body. Listen to what he wrote in Second Corinthians four, verses sixteen through eighteen:

"For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day. For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory; While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal."

Our outward man perishes. Some of us feel that every morning in our knees and our backs. But the inward man is renewed day by day. And Paul says to keep your eyes on the things that are not seen, because those are the things that last forever.

And in another place, in Romans eight, verse eighteen, he says:

"For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us."

Not worthy to be compared. All the tears, all the hospital rooms, all the empty chairs at the Thanksgiving table. One day, they'll seem so small next to the glory of that homecoming.

I sometimes picture it like this. You know how it is when the whole family comes home for the holidays. You hear the car doors slam in the driveway. You look out the window, and there they are, coming up the walk. And you hurry to the door before they even knock.

I believe heaven will be something like that, only a thousand times better. And the One standing at the door to welcome you will be Jesus Himself.
