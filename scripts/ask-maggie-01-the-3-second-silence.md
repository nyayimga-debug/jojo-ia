### 2. PARTIE 1 – CE QUI SE PASSE PENDANT LE SILENCE (3:00 – 7:30)

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
So, let's start with the big question. Who is actually calling you?

Most of us picture one crook sitting in a dark room, dialing numbers one at a time. I wish it were that simple. But that's not how it works anymore.

`[B-ROLL]` Grand open space de centre d'appels, rangées d'écrans et de casques (images d'archives génériques).

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
Most of these calls start with a computer program called an **auto-dialer**. Some are called "predictive dialers." And the idea is very simple. Instead of one person dialing one number, a computer dials *hundreds* of numbers at the same time, all day long.

`[GRAPHIQUE]` Un ordinateur au centre, des flèches vers des dizaines de téléphones. La plupart deviennent gris (« pas de réponse », « messagerie »). Un seul devient vert : « QUELQU'UN A DÉCROCHÉ ».

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
Think about it like a fisherman who casts a hundred lines into the lake at once. Most of those lines catch nothing. Some calls ring out. Some go to voicemail. Some numbers aren't even in service anymore.

But a few of those lines get a bite. A real person picks up. And that's you.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
Now here's the part nobody explains. The computer doesn't have enough people to talk to everybody it's calling. So when you pick up, the system first has to figure out two things.

`[TEXTE]` 1. Is this a real person… or a voicemail?
2. Is someone free to take this call?

**MAGGIE :**
Number one: is this a real, live human being, or an answering machine? These systems listen to the first sound you make. A short "Hello?" followed by silence usually means a real person. A long message like, "Hi, you've reached the Johnsons, leave a message," usually means voicemail.

Number two: once it decides you're real, it has to pass your call to whoever is free. Sometimes that's a person with a headset. Sometimes it's a recorded message.

And *that*, my friends, is your three seconds of silence. It's the computer listening to you, deciding you're worth a call, and handing you over.

`[B-ROLL]` Sablier qui s'écoule.

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
Now, I want to be fair here, because I always want to give you the truth and not just a scare. Not every call with a pause is a scam. Some legitimate companies, like telemarketers or appointment reminders, use the same kind of dialing software. So the pause doesn't *prove* a call is a scam.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
But here's my rule, and I want you to remember it. A pause means the caller is a machine that called a lot of people at once. And no one who truly needs *you*, your doctor, your daughter, your bank about something serious, calls you from a machine that dials thousands of people. They'll speak when you answer, or they'll leave a message.

So a pause is a yellow light. Slow down. Be careful.

`[TEXTE]` ⚠️ A pause = a yellow light. Slow down.

### 3. PARTIE 2 – CE QUE VOTRE « HELLO » LEUR APPREND (7:30 – 10:30)

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
Let's talk about what happens when you say "Hello"… and then say it again.

You might think, "Well, Maggie, I didn't say anything important. I just said hello."

And you're right, you didn't give away a secret. But you did give away something valuable.

`[GRAPHIQUE]` Une fiche avec le numéro de téléphone. Un tampon vert s'appose : « ACTIVE – REAL PERSON ANSWERS ».

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
You confirmed that your phone number is **real, working, and answered by a live person**. To a scammer, that's worth something. Numbers that get answered can be marked as "active," and lists of active numbers can be sold and traded between these operations.

So what happens next? More calls. Have you ever noticed that after you answer one strange call, it seems like three more show up that week? That's not your imagination.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
And when you say "Hello? Hello? Is anybody there?", you're also telling them something else. You're telling them you're *patient*. You're someone who stays on the line. You're polite. You're curious.

And scammers love polite, patient, curious people. Because those are exactly the people who will hold on long enough to hear the story they're about to tell.

`[TEXTE]` Your "Hello" tells them:
✓ Your number works
✓ A real person answers
✓ You stay on the line

**MAGGIE :**
So does that mean you should never answer the phone again? No, no. I'm not asking you to live like a hermit. We'll get to the smart way to answer in a little bit. For now, just remember: the second "Hello" is the one they're waiting for.

### 3 bis. « HOW DID THEY GET MY NUMBER? » (10:30 – 13:30)

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
Now, one of the questions I get asked the most is this one: "Maggie, I never gave my number to these people. How on earth did they get it?"

It's a great question. And the answer is a little uncomfortable, so let me walk you through it gently.

`[GRAPHIQUE]` Le numéro de téléphone au centre, avec quatre flèches qui arrivent : « Random dialing », « Data breaches », « People-search websites », « Forms & contests ».

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
**The first way is the simplest. They didn't need it.** Remember the auto-dialer? Many of these computers simply dial numbers in order. Every number in your area code, one after another. Your number wasn't chosen. It was just next in line.

**The second way is data breaches.** Over the years, many big companies, from stores to insurance companies to phone carriers, have had their customer files stolen by hackers. When that happens, names, phone numbers, emails, and sometimes even birth dates end up for sale in the dark corners of the internet. That's why a scammer may already know your name when they call. It feels personal. It isn't. They bought a list.

`[B-ROLL]` Gros titres génériques et flous sur des fuites de données (sans marque identifiable), cadenas brisé.

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
**The third way is people-search websites.** There are websites that collect public records, like property records and old phone books, and put them together into a profile: your name, your age range, your address, your relatives. Anyone can look them up.

**And the fourth way is forms and contests.** "Enter to win a free cruise!" "Fill out this survey for a $100 gift card!" Some of those exist mostly to collect phone numbers. The prize is you.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
So what can you do about it? You can't erase yourself from the internet completely, but you can make yourself a harder target:

`[TEXTE]` Make yourself a harder target:
✓ Don't enter "free prize" contests
✓ Think twice before writing your number on forms
✓ Ask a family member to help you request removal from people-search sites
✓ Assume a caller who knows your name is NOT proof they're real

**MAGGIE :**
And that last one is the most important. **A caller knowing your name, your address, or even the last four digits of your card is not proof that they're real.** That information is out there. The only proof is when *you* call *them* back, on a number *you* looked up yourself.

### 4. PARTIE 3 – LE PIÈGE « CAN YOU HEAR ME? » (13:30 – 16:30)

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
Now let's talk about a phrase you may have heard on one of these calls.

After the silence, a friendly voice comes on. Very cheerful. And the very first thing they say is…

`[B-ROLL]` Combiné de téléphone en gros plan, voix déformée.

**VOIX (effet téléphone) :** "Hi there! Can you hear me okay?"

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
And what do you say? Of course you say, "Yes." That's what any decent person says.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
For years, consumer groups and phone regulators have warned about this one. They call it the "Can you hear me?" call. The worry is that a scammer could record you saying "yes" and use that recording to claim you agreed to something, like a purchase or a subscription.

Now, here's where I'm going to be honest with you, because that's my promise on this channel. Investigators have had a hard time proving many cases where a recorded "yes" alone was used to steal money. A recording of one word isn't usually enough to empty a bank account.

`[TEXTE]` The real danger isn't the word "yes."
It's what comes AFTER.

**MAGGIE :**
But here's why I still want you to be careful. That question isn't really about the recording. It's a **door opener**. "Can you hear me?" Yes. "Is this the homeowner?" Yes. "Are you the person who handles the bills?" Yes. "And is this still your address on Maple Street?" Yes.

See what happened? In ten seconds, you've confirmed your phone number, that you own your home, that you handle the money, and your address. And you haven't asked them a single question about who *they* are.

`[GRAPHIQUE]` Une liste se remplit avec des coches : Phone ✓ · Homeowner ✓ · Handles money ✓ · Address ✓

**MAGGIE :**
So my advice is simple. On a call *you* didn't make, don't confirm anything about yourself until you know exactly who's calling. Instead of "yes," you can say:

`[TEXTE]` Instead of "Yes", say:
"Who is calling, please?"

**MAGGIE :**
"Who is calling, please?" It's polite. It's what your mother would have said. And it puts *you* in charge of the conversation.

### 5. PARTIE 4 – QUAND LA VOIX RESSEMBLE À CELLE D’UN PROCHE (16:30 – 20:30)

`[AVATAR]` Ton plus grave.

**MAGGIE :**
Now I want to talk about something newer, and I'll be honest, it's the one that worries me the most.

You've probably heard about artificial intelligence, AI. It can write letters, it can make pictures… and it can also **copy a person's voice**.

`[B-ROLL]` Ondes sonores à l'écran, puis le visage d'un jeune homme (stock) qui sourit dans une vidéo de réseau social.

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
With just a short recording, the kind of thing a young person posts on social media every day, today's tools can create a voice that sounds a lot like them. The Federal Trade Commission has publicly warned families about scammers using cloned voices to fake emergencies **(vérifier : alerte FTC « family emergency scams » et voice cloning, 2023)**.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
So picture this. Your phone rings. It's late. And you hear a voice that sounds just like your grandson.

`[B-ROLL]` Femme âgée au téléphone, l'air inquiet, de nuit.

**VOIX (effet téléphone, paniquée) :** "Grandma? It's me. I'm in trouble. I was in an accident, and the police are here. Please don't tell Mom. I need help."

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
Your heart drops. Every instinct you have says, "Help him." And that's exactly what they're counting on. Then another person comes on the line, a "lawyer" or a "police officer," and explains that bail money or a fee has to be paid *today*. In cash, by gift card, by wire, or sometimes they'll even send a "courier" to your door to pick up the money.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
Here's what I want you to notice. Even before AI, this scam worked without a cloned voice. Do you know why? Because the grandparent often fills in the name. "Michael? Is that you, Michael?" And now the caller knows exactly who to be.

So rule number one for family calls: **let them say their name first.** Don't hand it to them.

And rule number two is the best tool I know of. I want every family watching this to do it this week.

`[TEXTE]` 🔑 Create a FAMILY PASSWORD

**MAGGIE :**
A **family password**. Pick a word or a short phrase that only your family knows. Something silly, something no stranger would ever guess. The name of your first dog. "Purple pancakes." Whatever makes you smile.

And then you agree: if anyone ever calls in an emergency asking for money, you ask for the password. A real grandchild will know it, or will happily hang up so you can call them right back. A scammer won't.

`[B-ROLL]` Une famille autour d'une table qui rit ; une main écrit « Purple pancakes » sur un petit papier.

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
And the second step: **hang up and call back** on the number you already have for them. Not the number that just called you. Call your grandson's own cell phone. Call his mom. Nine times out of ten, you'll find him sitting safe at home, wondering why Grandma is so worried.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
No real emergency gets worse because you took two minutes to check. Two minutes. That's all it takes.

**MAGGIE :**
Now, I want to stop for a moment and say something important. If you've ever fallen for one of these calls, or come close, it does **not** mean you're foolish.

These operations fool doctors, teachers, engineers, people who ran businesses for forty years. Because these tricks aren't aimed at your intelligence. They're aimed at your **heart** and your **habits**.

`[TEXTE]` They don't target your mind.
They target your heart and your habits.

**MAGGIE :**
Let me show you the four buttons they push.

`[GRAPHIQUE]` Quatre gros boutons ronds, chacun s'allume quand Maggie en parle.

**MAGGIE (voix off) :**
**Button one: politeness.** Many of us were raised to answer every call, to never hang up on someone, and to be courteous to anyone who sounds official. Those are wonderful values. Scammers turn them into a weakness.

**Button two: fear.** "Your Social Security number has been suspended." "There's a warrant for your arrest." "Your account has been hacked." Fear shuts down the careful, thinking part of the brain and switches on the "do something now" part.

**Button three: urgency.** "You must act today." "Don't hang up or you'll lose everything." Urgency is how they stop you from doing the one thing that would save you: talking to someone you trust.

**Button four: authority.** They say they're from the government, from Medicare, from your bank's fraud department. They may even know your name and part of your address, because so much of our information has leaked online over the years.

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
And that silence at the beginning? It actually helps them. When you say "Hello" and nobody answers, you feel a little off balance. A little confused. So when a voice finally comes on, you feel *relieved*, and relief lowers your guard. That's why I call it the most powerful three seconds in the whole scam.

So here's what I want you to say to yourself whenever a call makes your heart race:

`[TEXTE]` "If it's urgent and scary… it's probably a scam."

**MAGGIE :**
"If it's urgent and scary, it's probably a scam." Say it with me. It might just be the most valuable sentence you hear all week.

### 7. PARTIE 6 – LES 6 APPELS QUI SUIVENT LE SILENCE (23:30 – 29:30)

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
All right. Now that you know how they find you, let's talk about what you'll hear *after* the silence. These are the six calls I hear about the most. For each one, I'll tell you what they say, and then what the *real* organization actually does.

`[TEXTE]` THE 6 CALLS THAT FOLLOW THE SILENCE

---

**`[TEXTE]` CALL #1 – "SOCIAL SECURITY"**

**VOIX (robotique) :** "This is the Social Security Administration. Your Social Security number has been suspended due to suspicious activity. Press 1 to speak with an officer."

**MAGGIE :**
Here's the truth. Your Social Security number **cannot be suspended**. It's not a driver's license. And the Social Security Administration has said clearly that it will never threaten you, never demand immediate payment, and never ask you to pay with gift cards, wire transfers, or cash by mail **(vérifier : page SSA « Protect yourself from scams »)**.

`[TEXTE]` ✓ The REAL Social Security: mostly contacts you by mail. Never threatens. Never asks for gift cards.

---

**`[TEXTE]` CALL #2 – "MEDICARE"**

**VOIX :** "Hello, this is Medicare. You're eligible for a new plastic Medicare card and a free back brace. I just need to verify your Medicare number."

**MAGGIE :**
Your Medicare number is just as precious as your Social Security number. Crooks use it to bill fake equipment and services in your name. And Medicare doesn't call out of the blue to ask for it. If you need a new card or you have a question, call the number on the back of the card you already have, or 1-800-MEDICARE **(vérifier)**.

`[TEXTE]` ✓ The REAL Medicare: won't call to ask for your Medicare number.

---

**`[TEXTE]` CALL #3 – "THE IRS"**

**VOIX :** "This is the final notice from the IRS. A lawsuit has been filed against you. Call back immediately to avoid arrest."

**MAGGIE :**
The IRS usually starts with a **letter in your mailbox**, not a threatening phone call. They don't call to threaten arrest, and they don't ask for payment by gift card or wire **(vérifier : page IRS sur les arnaques)**. If you're ever unsure, look up the official IRS number yourself and call them.

`[TEXTE]` ✓ The REAL IRS: sends a letter first. Never threatens arrest by phone.

---

**`[TEXTE]` CALL #4 – "YOUR BANK'S FRAUD DEPARTMENT"**

**VOIX (très professionnelle) :** "Hello, Mrs. Johnson, this is the fraud department at your bank. We've detected two suspicious charges. To protect your account, I'll need to verify your card number and PIN, and the code we just texted you."

**MAGGIE :**
This one sounds the most real, and that's why it's so dangerous. They might know your name and which bank you use. But listen closely. Your bank will **never** ask for your PIN. They will **never** ask you to read back a code they just texted you. And they will **never** ask you to move your money to a "safe account" to protect it.

`[TEXTE]` ✓ The REAL bank: NEVER asks for your PIN or the code they texted you. NEVER asks you to move money to "keep it safe."

**MAGGIE :**
That code they text you? That's the key to your front door. Never, ever read it to anyone who called you.

**`[TEXTE]` CALL #5 – "GRANDMA, I'M IN TROUBLE"**

**MAGGIE :**
We already talked about this one. Remember: let them say their name, ask for the family password, hang up and call back.

`[TEXTE]` ✓ Family password · Hang up · Call back on THEIR number

**`[TEXTE]` CALL #6 – "AMAZON / YOUR COMPUTER / YOUR UTILITY"**

**VOIX :** "This is Amazon. A $999 iPhone was ordered on your account. Press 1 to cancel."

**MAGGIE :**

### 9. PARTIE 8 – LES RÈGLES DU TÉLÉPHONE DE MAGGIE (33:30 – 37:00)

`[AVATAR]`

**MAGGIE :**
And now, as promised, here are **Maggie's Phone Rules**. Grab that notepad. I'd love for you to write these on a card and tape it right next to the phone, or on the fridge.

`[TEXTE]` 📋 MAGGIE'S PHONE RULES (affiché à l'écran, une règle à la fois, puis la liste complète)

**MAGGIE :**

**Rule number one: Let unknown numbers go to voicemail.** If it's important, they'll leave a message. Your doctor will. Your family will. Scammers usually won't.

**Rule number two: If you do answer and there's a pause, hang up.** You don't owe silence a conversation. It's not rude. It's smart.

**Rule number three: Answer with a question, not with your information.** Instead of "Yes, this is Margaret," say, "Who is calling, please?"

**Rule number four: Never give out codes, PINs, or passwords on a call you didn't make.** Not to your bank, not to Medicare, not to anyone.

**Rule number five: Hang up and call back.** Use the number on the back of your card, on your bill, or on the official website. Never the number the caller gives you.

**Rule number six: Never pay with gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, or cash to a courier.** If they ask, it's a scam. Every time.

**Rule number seven: Use your family password and talk to someone you trust before you send money.** A quick call to your son, your daughter, or a friend can save your life savings.

`[TEXTE]` (liste complète des 7 règles à l'écran pendant 10 secondes)
